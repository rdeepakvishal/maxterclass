The overtaking illusion

Counting overtakes measures bad qualifying

In 2023 — the most dominant season any driver has had in the modern era — Verstappen finished on the overtaking charts. The drivers above him weren't better racers; they simply started further back and had more cars in front of them to pass. Overtaking volume is a measure of where you started, not how well you raced.

Divide instead by the places each driver lost, and the picture inverts. Verstappen gave up four positions on track in the entire 2023 season.

The Comeback of the Lion

The control group

The one variable that holds

Every driver shares a garage, a budget and a car with exactly one other person. That teammate is the closest thing motorsport has to a controlled experiment: same machinery, same weekend, same track. So watch what happens when the wins fall off a cliff and the teammate comparison doesn't move.

2025 was called his worst season in five years. He out-qualified his teammates — Liam Lawson, then Yuki Tsunoda — in every one of the 24 rounds.

Qualifying head-to-head · 2025
The same variable, in seconds

And by how much

The head-to-head is a count — it says who was quicker, not by how far. Timing the two cars against each other in the same qualifying session, the margin settles at roughly half a second from 2019 onward and simply stays there: 0.54s in the 19-win season of 2023, 0.54s again in 2025, when he won eight. It does not track the results at all. 2026 is the one season that breaks the pattern.

The evidence on track

The drives that don't need a fast car

If the talent were fading, the recovery drives would fade first — they're the ones that depend least on machinery and most on racecraft. Three of his eight biggest comebacks have come in the last three seasons, including a win from seventeenth.

A grand slam is the rarest thing a driver can do across a weekend: take pole, lead every single lap, set the fastest lap, and win. Four boxes, one race, nothing left for anyone else.

Method & source

Classifying retirements. Ergast marks a retirement with positionText = "R". In this snapshot the 2026 rows also carry a numeric position for those retirements, while earlier seasons leave it null. Classifying on position would score a lap-zero engine failure as “finished 22nd” and corrupt every position-gain figure — 2026's net position change swings from −34 to +9 depending on which column you trust. Everything here classifies on positionText.

Poles. Counted as starting first on the grid, which is what public records report. Counting fastest-in-qualifying instead gives 51 rather than 48: five of his qualifying-fastest weekends carried a grid penalty (three of them Spa), and in 2021 the sprint winner was awarded pole. The grid-based count matches published season-by-season figures exactly.

Points. Championship points, so sprint points are included. Ergast keeps sprint results in a separate table; race points alone would understate 2023 as 530 rather than 575.

One gap in the source. The 2025 Qatar Grand Prix carries no grid data for any of the 20 drivers. That race is therefore excluded from average-grid and position-gain figures. Verstappen qualified third and won it, so it counts as a win away from pole.

Qualifying gap in seconds. Both laps are taken from the deepest qualifying session both cars ran — Q3 against Q3, Q1 against Q1 — and the season figure is the median across those weekends. Nothing is dropped for being a large gap; a weekend drops out only when one car set no usable time. An earlier version of this chart compared each driver's best lap of the weekend, which silently timed a Q3 lap against a Q1 lap whenever the teammate was knocked out early. Verstappen reached a deeper session than his teammate on 71% of 2025 weekends and 38% of 2024, so that method inflated exactly the seasons the story leaned on: 2025 read −0.805s where the like-for-like figure is −0.540s. The corrected series shows a margin that holds near half a second rather than one that widens.

The 2025 split. The season ran 24 rounds; the halves are rounds 1–12 and 13–24. Points include sprint points. The 2025 São Paulo comeback in the standout-drives chart is recorded by Ergast as a P19 start; he was knocked out in Q1 and actually started from the pit lane, so that grid-to-finish gain is a floor.

Grand slams. Pole, win, fastest lap and every lap led in the same race. The fastest lap is derived from the lap times rather than Ergast's rank column, which only exists from 2004 and would have hidden Senna, Mansell and most of Schumacher. Lap-by-lap data is complete from 1982, so nothing before that is countable — Jim Clark's record eight are outside it. Races under 20 racing laps are excluded: the 2021 Belgian Grand Prix was abandoned behind the safety car after a single lap and would otherwise satisfy “led every lap” trivially, handing Verstappen a seventh slam that no record book counts.

Overtakes. There is no overtake column in this data. Places gained and lost are derived from lap-by-lap classified position, with each pit in-lap and the following out-lap dropped so that a pit stop is never counted as a pass. It remains a proxy: it cannot separate a pass on track from a rival's slow lap.

Not included. No telemetry — this dataset carries timing and classification only, no throttle, brake or speed traces.
